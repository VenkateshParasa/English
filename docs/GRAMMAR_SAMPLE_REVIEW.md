# Grammar sample review — US-500 spike

**What this is.** One grammar point, authored end to end at the standard all 24 would need, so you
can decide whether Sprint 5 (33 points) is worth committing to. Point chosen: **3 — articles
(a / an / the / zero)**, because Telugu has no articles at all (`T-G1`, priority M),
`CURRICULUM.md` calls it "the classic Indian-English pressure point", and it is genuinely hard to
teach — a flattering point would not have tested anything.

Content: `data/grammar.js` (verifies clean; see §6). Read §1 as teaching, §3 as the honest part.

## 1. The authored point, as a learner would meet it

### a, an, the — and when to use nothing at all
Foundation · A1–A2 · syllabus point 3 · SRS key `gram:articles`

**The rule** (one sentence, and the exact string shown on a wrong answer)

> Before you name a thing, ask what your listener already knows: use **the** when you both have the
> same one in mind, **a** or **an** when you are introducing one they do not know about yet, and
> nothing at all when you mean the thing in general or an amount you cannot count.

**Why it works this way**

> Every time you name a thing in English, you also have to say whether your listener can already
> pick it out. That is the entire job of these little words: **a** hands your listener something
> new, **the** points at something you have both got in mind already — from earlier in the
> conversation, or just from the room you are standing in. Telugu marks this only when it matters,
> so in English the slot in front of the noun feels empty rather than wrong, and that is why this
> error outlives almost every other one. Most learners need a long time before it becomes
> automatic; what you are building here is a habit, not a fact.

**How to decide, in real time**

1. Can I count this thing, and am I talking about one of them? If it is uncountable (water, advice,
   money) or a general plural (buses, doctors), you usually need nothing at all.
2. Does my listener already know which one I mean — because we just mentioned it, or because it is
   obvious right here? If yes, use **the**.
3. One thing, and they do not know which yet? Use **a**, or **an** if the next word starts with a
   vowel *sound*.

**Why it matters** (deliberately not inflated)

> A missing article rarely stops anyone understanding you, so this is not about sounding correct —
> it is about two specific things: it is the most noticeable feature of Indian English to a listener
> from outside the region, and a wrong **the** can genuinely mislead, because "He is the engineer"
> sends your listener looking for an engineer they were supposed to already know about.

### Notice

> **You:** Excuse me, is there **a** chemist near here?
> **Passer-by:** Yes — go past **the** bank and it is on **the** left.
> **You:** Thanks. Do they take **cards**?

*Why is it "a chemist" but "the bank", when neither has been mentioned before?*
Because **the** is not about mentioning — it is about whether you both already know which one. You
do not know which chemist you want, so it is **a chemist**. But there is only one bank on that
street and you can both see it, so it is **the bank**. And *cards* is a general plural — all cards,
not particular ones — so it takes nothing at all.

### Three contrast pairs

Both members of every pair are correct English. Only the article differs, and the meaning changes.

| | |
|---|---|
| I am waiting for **a** bus. | Any bus that goes my way. I have not told you which one, and you do not need to know. |
| I am waiting for **the** bus. | The one we both know about — my usual one, or the one we were just talking about. |

> Both are correct. **a** means "you do not know which one yet"; **the** means "we are both thinking
> of the same one".

| | |
|---|---|
| He is **a** doctor. | That is his job. This is the normal way to say what someone does. |
| He is **the** doctor. | He is the particular doctor we are expecting — the one who will see you, the one on duty. You would say it while pointing him out. |

> Jobs take **a** or **an**. **The** picks out one specific person your listener is already looking
> for — which is why "He is the engineer" makes people ask "which engineer?".

| | |
|---|---|
| I am going to school. | I am going as a student, for lessons. Here *school* is the activity, not the building. |
| I am going to **the** school. | I am going to the building — to meet a teacher, to vote, to collect my nephew. |

> With *school*, *work*, *hospital*, *church* and *prison*, English drops the article when you mean
> the activity and uses **the** when you mean the building. This is a short fixed list, not a
> general rule.

### How the articles actually sound

> Nobody stresses these words, so do not give them their full value. **a** is /ə/ ("uh"), not /eɪ/
> ("ay") — /ə ˈbʌs/, not /eɪ ˈbʌs/. **the** is /ðə/ before a consonant sound (*the bus*) and /ði/
> before a vowel sound (*the office*). **an** links straight into the next word, so *an hour* comes
> out as one word, /əˈnaʊə/. If you say these words clearly and separately, you sound slower and
> less fluent than you actually are.

### Six practice items

Options are always the same four: **a**, **an**, **the**, — (no article). Only the article varies.

| # | Item | Accepted | Isolates |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | It takes ___ hour by bus. | **an** | a/an goes by *sound*, not letter |
| 2 | I watched a film last night. ___ film was really long. | **the** | second mention |
| 3 | I went to ___ shop and bought milk. | **the** *and* **a** | the empty slot (T-G1) |
| 4 | I am vegetarian. I do not eat ___ meat. | *no article* | things in general |
| 5 | Could you close ___ door behind you? | **the** | *the* from the situation, with no earlier mention |
| 6 | I usually go to ___ work by bus. | *no article* | fixed phrases with no rule underneath |

Every wrong option on every item carries a reason, a contrast pair, and a retry cue. Two examples
of what a wrong answer actually returns:

**Item 3, learner leaves the gap empty** — the T-G1 error itself:

> This is the T-G1 pattern exactly: in Telugu *shop* can stand there alone, and in English it
> cannot. *Shop* is one countable thing, so the slot in front of it has to be filled — with **the**
> if it is the shop we both know, with **a** if it is just some shop.
> · I went to **the** shop and bought milk.
> · I went shopping and bought milk.
> **Try again:** if you cannot decide between a and the, notice that *I went shopping* avoids the
> problem completely — that is a legitimate escape, not cheating. But here, which shop do I mean?

**Item 6, learner picks "the"** — real English, different meaning, and it is told so:

> **The work** means the tasks themselves, not the place or the routine — so "I go to the work"
> leaves your listener waiting to hear which work. *Go to work* belongs to the same small family as
> *go to school*, *go to bed*, *go home*: activity, no article.
> · I usually go to work by bus.
> · I finished **the** work you sent me last night.
> **Try again:** do I mean the daily routine of being at my job, or a specific pile of tasks?

Item 3 accepts **both** *the* and *a*, and shows the difference between them even when the learner
is right — see §2.3.

### Say it aloud

> Out loud, in three sentences, tell me about the last thing you bought. Introduce it in the first
> sentence with **a** or **an**. In the next two sentences, talk about the same thing using **the**.
> *(45 seconds. Skippable — speaking is never a gate.)*

Self-check: Did I say **a/an** the very first time and **the** every time after? · Did I put
something in front of every singular noun, with no empty slots? · Did I keep the articles short and
weak ("uh", "thuh")? · Did I speak for the whole time without stopping to plan?

Model, with the note attached to it: *"Last week I bought a water bottle. The bottle was only two
hundred rupees. I keep the bottle in my bag now, so I drink more water."* — **Read this once to see
the pattern, then look away from the screen and say your own. Reading it aloud is not the exercise.**

### The honest caveats, shown to the learner

- Some phrases simply have no article and are learned whole: *go to work*, *go to bed*, *at home*,
  *by bus*, *on foot*, *have breakfast*. There is no rule to derive.
- British and American English disagree about hospital: *in hospital* and *in the hospital* are both
  standard. This app will not mark either wrong.
- Names are inconsistent: *India*, *Hyderabad*, *Lake Superior* take nothing; *the UK*, *the US*,
  *the Netherlands*, *the Ganges* take **the**.
- Articles are the last thing most advanced speakers get fully right, and a real minority of uses
  are idiom rather than logic. Where careful speakers would accept either, this app accepts both —
  **if you are ever told a defensible answer is wrong, the item is broken, not you.**

### Telugu note (`T-G1`, priority M)

> Telugu has no articles, so there is nothing to translate from. Where it matters, Telugu uses other
> tools: *oka* (ఒక, "one") to introduce something new, and *ā* (ఆ, "that") or *ī* (ఈ, "this") to
> point at something known. Most of the time it uses neither, because the listener can work it out.
> English does not allow that: in front of a singular countable noun the slot must be filled, every
> single time, even when the meaning is already obvious. That is why the error is so persistent —
> you are not choosing the wrong word, you are not hearing a slot that your first language does not
> have.
>
> **Bridge:** if you would say *oka*, English almost certainly wants **a/an**; if you would say *ā*,
> English wants **the**. The third habit has no Telugu equivalent and needs deliberate practice:
> when you would say nothing at all in Telugu, English still needs **a** or **the** before a
> singular countable noun.

## 2. Design decisions

**2.1 The rule is about the listener, not about "specific things".**
"Use *the* for specific things" is the standard formulation and it does not work, because a learner
saying "I went to shop" thinks their shop is extremely specific. Definiteness is not a property of
the thing; it is a claim about **shared knowledge between speaker and listener**. Framing it that
way makes *the bank* (never mentioned, but visible), *the film* (mentioned a second ago) and *the
door behind you* (identified by the situation) one rule instead of three, and it gives the learner a
question they can actually ask mid-sentence. `decide` turns it into a three-step procedure, because
a rule you cannot run in real time is knowledge, not habit — which is precisely the learner
`CURRICULUM.md §1` describes.

**2.2 Contrast means contrast, not "wrong vs right".**
`CONTENT_AUTHORING_GUIDE.md §6` sketches `contrast: [{right, wrong}]`. I did not use that shape.
A right/wrong pair teaches which form to avoid; it cannot teach what the form *does*. All six
sentences in the three pairs are correct English, differing only in the article, with a real change
of meaning — that is what makes the article visible as a carrier of meaning rather than as a tax on
sentences. Genuine learner errors are not lost: they moved to a `commonErrors` field
(*"I went to shop"*, *"He is the engineer"*), keyed to their `T-G` code and shown **after** practice
so the lesson does not prime the error it is trying to remove. **This means the authoring guide's
grammar schema needs updating** — I could not edit it here.

**2.3 One item deliberately has two right answers.**
Item 3 accepts both *the shop* and *a shop*, and tells the learner the difference even when they are
right. This is the pedagogical centre of the lesson: articles are a meaning choice, not a lottery,
and an app that accepts only one answer teaches the opposite. It also flushes out a small tension in
the specs — `TEACHING_METHODOLOGY.md §2` says a right first answer should "say nothing more", but
saying nothing after a two-answer item teaches that the two are interchangeable. Resolved with an
explicit `showDifferenceOnCorrect` flag, true only when `accept.length > 1`, and asserted as such.

**2.4 Most wrong article choices are real English, and the feedback says so.**
Every feedback entry carries `grammaticalButDifferent`. Where it is true (*a bus* for *the bus*,
*the meat* for *meat*), the feedback shows what the learner's sentence would have meant instead of
implying they broke a rule. For a nervous adult this is the difference between a lesson and a
reprimand, and it is also just true.

**2.5 Mistake categories: adopted the live taxonomy, kept the finer grain.**
While this spike was being written, `js/core/mistakes.js` landed with a real taxonomy — dotted ids
(`gram.articles`, code `T-G1`, `drill: { strand: 'grammar', target: 'articles' }`), not the kebab
names sketched in `IMPLEMENTATION_PLAN.md` Phase 9. Two things follow:

- **Content now references the live ids** and `MISTAKE_CATEGORIES` in `data/grammar.js` is the
  grammar subset of them, held there only so a typo fails a test instead of silently landing in
  `general.uncategorised`. `MISTAKE_CATEGORY_ALIASES` maps the plan's old names to the shipped ones,
  so the plan stays readable against the code. The verification harness parses
  `js/core/mistakes.js` and fails if any `logAs` is not a live id.
- **That file merges article omission and wrong article choice into one learner-facing category**
  ("Missing or wrong a/an/the"), on the stated grounds that *"article omission, 4.7 times"* is not a
  sentence anyone should be handed. I agree, and dropped my earlier proposal for a separate
  `article-wrong-choice` category. The distinction is not lost: every feedback entry carries
  `errorKind` (`omission` / `wrong-choice` / `uncountable-treated-as-countable`), which is authoring
  and analysis data and is never displayed.
- **One genuine gap in the live taxonomy:** there is no category for *treating an uncountable noun as
  countable with an article* (*"a work"*, *"a meat"*). `gram.uncountable-plural` is specifically
  plural `-s` (*"informations"*), so it would misdescribe the error. I log those to `gram.articles`,
  which is defensible — it is an article-slot error — but the owner of `js/core/mistakes.js` may want
  a row for it. Happily, their `drill.target: 'articles'` already equals this lesson's `id`, so
  drill-from-mistake works with no mapping layer.

**2.6 Zero article is a first-class value, not an absence.**
`ZERO_ARTICLE = ""` with a separate `ZERO_ARTICLE_LABEL`. Rendering the answer into the gap then
produces the correct sentence with no special-casing, and "no article" becomes a thing the learner
actively chooses rather than a thing they forget.

**2.7 Fields the SRS and the mistake log will need are already there.**
`srsType: 'gram'`, `srsRef`, `srsKey: 'gram:articles'`, `mistakeCategory`, per-feedback `logAs`, and
a `review` block (`rulePrompt` + rotating `itemIds`) so a *due* review is a one-line reminder plus
two items — not the whole lesson re-taught, and not always the same first item.
**One integration note, and it will bite `US-506`:** Phase 4's SRS generalisation has landed
concurrently, and `PROJECTORS.gram` in `js/core/srs.js` is
`['id', 'title', 'explanation', 'example', 'practice', 'difficulty']`. Everything not in that list is
silently dropped from the stored review card — and `explanation` and `example` exist in neither this
content nor the authoring guide's sketch, while `rule` and `review`, the two fields a review card
actually needs, are not projected at all. A grammar review would currently render a title and six
items with no rule to show on a wrong answer. The needed list is written into the file's header, in
an `SRS PROJECTION CONTRACT` block:
`['id', 'title', 'cefr', 'rule', 'explain', 'contrast', 'practice', 'review', 'mistakeCategory', 'difficulty']`.
This must be fixed on the projector side, not by duplicating paragraphs under two names.
(The good news: `RENDERABLE.gram` is now `d => !!d`, so the old `data.quiz` filter no longer hides
`gram:` items.)

**2.8 Every field is a template decision, not a local one.**
`mode` is present with `'gap'` and reserves `'choose' | 'repair' | 'order'`, so points 6 (question
formation) and 14 (reported speech) need no schema change. `focus` per item makes the six items
addressable individually. `fallbackFeedback` exists so a typed answer outside the option set still
returns reason + contrast + retry, rather than a bare verdict — FR-GRM-2 has no exception for free
text. `prerequisites` records that articles genuinely depend on point 4 (see §5).

## 3. Honest self-assessment

### 3.1 The real weakness: six items cannot cover articles

`FR-GRM-1` fixes the shape at 6 practice items. Articles have at least six *sub-rules*, so my six
items are one shallow pass over each: a/an by sound, second mention, the empty slot, generic zero,
situational *the*, fixed phrases. That is breadth where drilling needs depth, and it half-violates
the authoring guide's own "exactly one thing varies" — it holds within each item, not across the
set. A teacher would want 15–20 items here, or the point split into 3a (a/an/the, the shared-
knowledge engine) and 3b (zero article: generics, uncountables, fixed phrases).
**Recommendation:** make 6 items a *minimum*, and let the three or four highest-frequency points
carry 12. It costs about 40% more authoring on four points, not on all 24.

### 3.2 Things I am not sure about

- **The Telugu content needs a native speaker.** *oka* (ఒక), *ā* (ఆ), *ī* (ఈ) and the claim that
  Telugu marks definiteness "only when it matters" are the weakest-sourced statements in the file.
  The claim "Telugu has no articles" follows `REQUIREMENTS.md T-G1`, but *oka* is arguably a
  quasi-article and a linguist might call my framing too tidy. This is the one part I would not ship
  unchecked.
- **The IPA is from knowledge, not from a dictionary.** `CONTENT_AUTHORING_GUIDE.md §2` rule 1 says
  source it from a dictionary or omit it — I had no dictionary access. `/əˈnaʊə/`, `/ðə ˈʃɒp/`,
  `/ɡəʊ tə ˈwɜːk/`, `/ðə ˈdɔː/` need verifying before release.
- **The transcriptions are British (RP), and the app's TTS voice is `en-US`.** *shop* as /ʃɒp/ and
  *door* as /dɔː/ will not match what the learner hears. I chose RP because Indian English is
  historically closer to it, but the app should pick one and be consistent across all content —
  a decision above this file's pay grade.
- **Prompts use full forms** (*I am*, *do not*) with contractions only in the `spoken` fields. For a
  spoken-English app the reverse is arguable. `data.js` is inconsistent today, so I followed the
  more conservative option; happy to flip it, but it should be a project-wide rule before 23 more
  points are written.
- **Two items run to two clauses** (items 3, 4) against the guide's "clause count: 1" for
  foundation. Second-mention *the* cannot be taught in one clause. Every sentence is within the
  4–8-word cap; the clause rule needs an explicit exception for grammar items.

### 3.3 Where a qualified English teacher would disagree with me

1. **Contrast pair 3 is not A1–A2.** *going to school* vs *going to the school* is a B1 subtlety,
   and it is BrE-flavoured — many American teachers would say *going to the school* is normal for any
   purpose. It is included because Indian English does this differently and it is high-frequency,
   but a teacher could fairly call it a level violation and cut it.
2. **Accepting *a shop* in item 3.** A teacher could argue *I went to the shop* is so overwhelmingly
   the default that accepting *a shop* teaches a false equivalence. I think `FR-GRM-5` settles it —
   *a shop* is unambiguously correct English in that sentence — but the objection is legitimate and
   the fix would be to add context forcing *the*.
3. **My rule sentence leads with *the*; my `decide` procedure leads with countability.** Two
   different orderings of the same material, thirty lines apart. Some teachers teach articles
   strictly countability-first (a flowchart); some teach definiteness-first. I have half of each,
   and a reviewer may find that incoherent. Defence: the rule is for the wrong-answer moment (one
   sentence, must land instantly), the procedure is for planning speech. It is still a fair hit.
4. **"Articles rarely stop anyone understanding you"** in `whyItMatters` could be read as licensing
   the error. I think it is true, it mirrors the `T-P12` intelligibility-not-accent posture, and a
   nervous adult needs to hear it — but a teacher aiming at exam accuracy would strike it.
5. **Item 5 accepts only *the*.** *Could you close a door behind you?* is constructible in a
   many-doored room. I added "behind you" specifically to close that gap and I believe it is now
   shut, but a pedant might not agree.

## 4. Adversarial pass: is there a second correct answer I have not listed?

This is where grammar items break, so I ran every item deliberately looking for the answer I had
called wrong. Three items changed as a result.

| Item | What I found | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 3 · "I went to ___ shop" | **Both *the* and *a* are correct.** *the shop* = the usual one; *a shop* = some shop. My first draft accepted only *the*. | Accepts both, each with its meaning, and shows the difference on a correct answer. |
| 4 · "I do not eat ___ meat" | Bare, this admits ***the***: "I do not eat the meat" is fine when standing in front of a specific dish. | Prepended **"I am vegetarian."**, which forces the generic reading. *the* is now genuinely wrong here, and the feedback explains why using exactly that restaurant scenario. |
| 5 · "Could you close ___ door" | ***a door*** is defensible in a room with several open doors. | Added **"behind you"**, which identifies the door uniquely. *a* is now wrong, and its feedback still concedes the multi-door reading rather than pretending it is impossible. |
| 1 · "It takes ___ hour by bus" | *a hour* is non-standard; *the hour* has no reading here (the idiom is *within the hour*). Clean. | No change. Kept short — *by bus* also usefully takes no article. |
| 2 · second mention | *a film* is grammatical but incoherent as a continuation; *that film* would work but is not an option. | No change; `fallbackFeedback` explicitly concedes *that*. |
| 6 · "go to ___ work" | *the work* / *a work* have no reading in this frame. Clean — but it is a fixed phrase, so the item says so rather than pretending a rule produces it. | No change. |

Two by-products worth keeping:
- Item 4's prompt made me notice that ***I am vegetarian*** and ***I am a vegetarian*** are **both**
  correct — an article question inside my own prompt. Rather than hide it, the item's `alsoNotice`
  now teaches it as a place where the choice is genuinely free.
- Every `fallbackFeedback` concedes the near-miss determiners a learner might type (*my*, *that*,
  *any*, *one*) and explains that they do the same job, instead of rejecting them.

Machine-checked, not just eyeballed: the verification harness fails the build if any `feedback`
entry targets an answer that also appears in `accept` — i.e. if an item ever marks its own
defensible answer wrong.

## 5. Estimate for the remaining 23 points

**Per point, at this standard:** 2.5–4 hours. Roughly 45 min on the rule, mechanism and contrast
pairs; 90 min on six items with per-wrong-answer feedback (this is the bulk — 15 or so feedback
entries per point, each needing a reason, a real contrast pair and a retry cue); 30 min adversarial
pass; 30 min production task, caveats and L1 note. The schema cost is now paid once.

**Total: 70–90 hours** of focused authoring, ≈ 2–2.5 working weeks, excluding review. Against
`US-502/503/504` at 5 + 5 + 5 = 15 story points for 23 points of content, that looks optimistic
unless a story point is ~6 hours for you. **The estimate to challenge is not the schema work, it is
the feedback writing** — six items is 90 minutes, but *good* wrong-answer feedback for six items is
most of the point.

**Materially harder than articles:**

| Point | Why |
|---|---|
| 9 · present perfect vs past simple | The worst case for `FR-GRM-5`: nearly every gap admits both *I ate* and *I have eaten*. Contrast pairs are easy; six items where exactly one answer is right needs heavy context engineering, and BrE/AmE diverge (*Did you eat yet?*). Budget double. |
| 12 · gerund vs infinitive | Not a rule, a verb list. Six items sample a lexicon; needs a `verbList` field the schema does not yet have. |
| 20 · phrasal verbs by particle | Huge lexical surface, and separability judgements vary between speakers. Probably needs splitting by particle. |
| 21 · discourse markers · 23 · hedging | Pragmatic, not grammatical: the "wrong" answers are usually just less apt. Gap-fill cannot grade this honestly — needs a `mode: 'choose'` "which is more appropriate here, and why" item type, and the feedback has to argue rather than rule. |
| 22 · clefts and fronting · 24 · inversion, subjunctive remnants, articles with abstract nouns | C1. Alternatives are almost always defensible, and learner-plausible sentences are much harder to invent than at A2. |
| 14 · reported speech | Backshift is frequently optional, so most items admit two answers by nature. |
| 15 · passive, "and when not to use it in speech" | The interesting half is a judgement call, which is not gradeable — will need a rubric/production item, not practice items. |

**Easier than articles:** 1 (`be`), 4 (countable/uncountable), 5 (past simple), 6 (question
formation), 7 (prepositions), 11 (comparatives). Closed answer sets, low ambiguity, and 4/6 have a
`T-G` code to aim at — expect 2 hours each.

## 7. Specification problems found

1. **`FR-GRM-1`'s fixed "6 practice items" fights the high-frequency points.** Articles need 12–18;
   points 21 and 23 arguably need fewer but of a different type. Suggest "≥6".
2. **The syllabus orders articles (3) before countable/uncountable (4), but you cannot choose
   a/the/zero without countability.** Every one of my items leans on it and two log mistakes as
   `countable-uncountable`. Either swap 3 and 4, or accept that point 3 carries a countability
   primer. Recorded as `prerequisites: ["countable-uncountable"]` in the file.
3. **`CONTENT_AUTHORING_GUIDE.md §6`'s `contrast: [{right, wrong}]` contradicts the methodology.**
   The guide should be updated to the `{pair: [{text, means}], takeaway}` shape, or the two
   documents will keep producing different content.
4. **The guide's foundation limits (4–8 words, 1 clause) are unachievable for grammar items** that
   need two mentions of the same noun. Needs an explicit exception, or grammar prompts get their own
   row in the §1 table.
5. **The mistake-log vocabulary has no row for "uncountable treated as countable with an article"**
   (*"a work"*, *"a meat"*) — `gram.uncountable-plural` is plural `-s` only. See §2.5; currently
